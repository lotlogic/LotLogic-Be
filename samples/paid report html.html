Existing house
Position Middle of block
Footprint 194 m²
The house sits in the middle of the block, which is the single biggest constraint on this site.

A centrally positioned house leaves limited usable space both in front and behind. Adding a second dwelling while keeping the existing house would require careful design to meet setback requirements and still achieve a workable floor plan for the new dwelling.

For most redevelopment scenarios — dual occupancy or unit titling — the existing house would likely need to be removed or substantially altered. The footprint of 194 m² represents 28% of the total block area, which limits what can be built around it.

Rear yard space
Assessment Moderate
Depth 10.5 m
10.5 m of rear yard depth is workable for a granny flat, but there is limited margin.

ACT planning rules require a minimum 3 m setback from the rear boundary. That leaves approximately 7.5 m of buildable depth — enough for a granny flat up to 90 m² if the width of the lot cooperates. However, with trees at the front and a central house position, the effective available space is more constrained than the raw depth suggests.

A granny flat in this rear yard is technically possible but would require careful site planning to confirm setbacks, tree protection zones, and access can all be satisfied simultaneously.

Site coverage
Maximum allowed 344 m² (50%)
Existing house 194 m²
Remaining allowance 150 m²
There is a remaining building allowance of approximately 150 m² — enough for a granny flat, but not much more while the existing house remains.

Under current rules, buildings can cover a maximum of 50% of the block. Your block of 688 m² allows up to 344 m² of building footprint in total. The existing house already uses 194 m² of that allowance.

If the existing house were removed, the full 344 m² becomes available — significantly more room to design two well-proportioned dwellings.

Trees
Significant trees visible 3 or more
Location Front of block
Three or more significant trees are visible at the front of the block. Their protection zones will affect what can be built and where.

Trees in the ACT are protected under the Urban Forest Act 2023 if they meet certain size criteria (8 m+ tall, 8 m+ canopy width, or 1 m+ trunk circumference at 1.4 m height). Protected trees have a tree protection zone extending 4 m from the trunk in all directions — no building, excavation, or root disturbance is permitted within this zone without approval.

With trees positioned at the front, this is likely to affect any driveway reconfiguration and the buildable envelope near the street. Whether the trees are formally registered or simply meet the protection criteria is something a detailed review would confirm.

Heritage
Heritage overlay None
No heritage constraints apply to this block — a positive result for redevelopment flexibility.

Based on ACT Government mapping, your property is not within a heritage overlay. This means there are no heritage-related restrictions on demolishing, altering, or extending the existing house, and no constraints on new structures from a heritage perspective.

Heritage overlays can significantly complicate development approvals and add cost. The absence of any heritage designation on this block removes one of the more difficult planning constraints to work around.

Easements
Easements identified None visible
No easements appear to run through the body of this block — a favourable result for development flexibility.

Easements are legal rights that allow utilities or services (sewer, stormwater, electrical) to run through private land. If an easement runs through the middle of a block, it restricts where you can build — structures cannot be built over or too close to an easement without approval, and in many cases approval is not possible.

The mapping does not show any easements cutting through this block. A formal title search and survey would confirm this definitively before committing to any design.

Sewer
Connection location Rear of block
Sewer at the rear is a common configuration in Canberra — but it adds a cost consideration if a second dwelling is positioned toward the front.

For a second dwelling or subdivision, each dwelling typically needs its own sewer connection. If the new dwelling is at the front or middle of the block, extending the sewer line from the rear connection can add cost and complexity to the build.

This is not a deal-breaker, but it is a factor in the design and cost planning for any dual occupancy or unit titling outcome. An engineer would confirm the connection path as part of any detailed feasibility.

Driveway and access
Frontage 23 m
Second driveway Unlikely as-is
The 23 m frontage is borderline for fitting a second independent driveway — particularly with trees at the front.

For unit titling or dual occupancy with separately accessed dwellings, ACT rules generally require each dwelling to have independent driveway access. Standard driveway widths plus the required separation make this difficult on a 23 m frontage once the existing driveway and front trees are factored in.

Alternative configurations — a shared driveway, or a battle-axe layout where one dwelling accesses via a side leg — may solve this, but would need to be tested against the specific design. This is one of the key items a detailed feasibility would resolve.
